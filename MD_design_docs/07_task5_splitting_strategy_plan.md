# Task 5.4 — Train/Test Splitting Strategy for the Lysis/MIC Transfer-Learning Model

*Implementation-level companion to `02_peptideclm_transfer_learning_plan.md` §5.4 ("Splits (non-negotiable)") and §6.1 (cyclic/linear domain-shift concern). That doc states the conclusion — homology-aware or embedding-cluster splits, not random — and cites Feller & Wilke and QMAP as precedent. This doc verifies exactly what those two precedents did (primary sources, not secondhand paraphrase), and turns the recommendation into a concrete, executable pipeline for the HC50/MIC dataset from §5.3.*

---

## 0. TL;DR

1. **Feller & Wilke's split is a genuine leave-one-cluster-out (LOCO) *cross-validation* scheme**, not a single fixed holdout: 6 k-means clusters over PCA-reduced, pretrained-model embeddings, each cluster rotates through as the fully-held-out test set, and for each rotation the remaining 5 clusters are used to train 5 models (5-fold CV for early stopping / checkpoint selection), whose predictions on the held-out cluster are mean-pooled. That's up to 30 fine-tuning runs to fully reproduce their protocol. **Verified directly from the paper's Methods text and from the authors' own notebook code** (`AaronFeller/PeptideCLM` repo) — not just secondary summaries.
2. **QMAP's split is homology-graph partitioning, not CD-HIT/MMseqs2.** It computes exact global (Needleman-Wunsch, BLOSUM45) pairwise sequence identity, builds a similarity graph at a **60% identity threshold**, partitions it with the **Leiden community-detection algorithm**, and randomly assigns whole communities to the test set (≈20% of data) — then post-filters any remaining training sequence too similar to any test sequence. They generate **5 independent test sets** (different random seeds) rather than a rotating CV scheme. This is confirmed from the full preprint text and the released code (`anthol42/QMAP`).
3. **For this project:** cluster on PeptideCLM-2's own **mean-pooled final-layer embeddings** (this matches PeptideCLM-2's own convention, which differs from v1's CLS-token convention — see §1.1 and §3.1), run a **sequence-identity graph partition as an independent cross-check**, and — this is the one hard new requirement the source doc didn't spell out — **split at the peptide level over the union of the HC50 and MIC datasets before deriving per-task train/test sets**, because §5.1's shared-encoder multitask architecture makes a peptide's cluster assignment binding across both heads, not just its own label.
4. **Given likely small n (HC50 ≈ hundreds–low thousands, MIC ≈ low thousands, per QMAP's own DBAASP pull: 18,033 filtered peptides, ~4,000 with *E. coli* MIC, ~800 with HC50)**, recommend a **cheaper variant of Feller & Wilke's scheme**: 4–6 outer LOCO clusters (k chosen via silhouette/DB/CH exactly as they did, but biased low given small n), each with a *single* fixed validation cluster for early stopping rather than a full inner 5-fold — cutting compute ~5x versus the exact original recipe while keeping the leakage-safe property, which is the property that actually matters.
5. **Validate, don't assume:** after splitting, reproduce QMAP's own diagnostic — plot the max-identity(test → train) distribution and compare it against QMAP's own published reference numbers (homology split: median 50%, 90th pctile 57.1%; random split: median 90%, 90th pctile 100%). Also reproduce their threshold-sensitivity experiment (linear probe PCC vs. identity threshold) directly on this project's data, because QMAP's leakage-inflation numbers are for *their* dataset/task, not proof of the same magnitude here.

## 1. Feller & Wilke's exact methodology (verified from primary sources)

**Sources used, both fetched and read directly (not taken from search-engine summaries):**
- Paper: Feller, A. & Wilke, C.O., *J. Chem. Inf. Model.* 2025, 65, 571–579. DOI `10.1021/acs.jcim.4c01441`. Free full text: PMC11971985 (`https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11971985/`).
- Code: `github.com/AaronFeller/PeptideCLM`, specifically `CycPeptMPDB_clustering_and_analysis.ipynb`, fetched and read cell-by-cell.

### 1.1 Embedding used for clustering

- **Model:** the **pretrained** PeptideCLM checkpoint (MLM objective only, before PAMPA fine-tuning) — a RoFormer with a custom SMILES-Pair-Encoding tokenizer (`SMILES_SPE_Tokenizer`).
- **Representation, confirmed directly from their code:**
  ```python
  outputs = model(input_ids, attention_mask, return_dict=True, output_hidden_states=True)
  cls_embeddings.append(outputs.hidden_states[-1][0][0])
  ```
  This is the **first-token ("CLS-style") embedding of the final hidden layer**, one vector per SMILES — **not mean pooling.** This is an important, easy-to-miss detail: it directly contradicts an assumption someone porting "the Feller & Wilke recipe" might make if they only read the paper's prose (which just says "generated embeddings... using the pretrained PeptideCLM" without specifying pooling). The paper's Methods text independently confirms PCA was run on "embeddings of all peptides with PAMPA scores using the pretrained PeptideCLM," consistent with this code.

### 1.2 Dimensionality reduction + clustering algorithm

- **PCA** to the minimum number of components retaining **99% of variance** — landed at **153 components** for their CycPeptMPDB PAMPA subset.
- **K-means** (confirmed exact algorithm — `sklearn.cluster.KMeans`, not MiniBatchKMeans, though they benchmarked both plus `AgglomerativeClustering` across k=2–10 before settling on plain KMeans).
- **k = 6**, chosen by jointly optimizing **high silhouette score, low Davies-Bouldin Index, and high Calinski-Harabasz Index** (paper Methods, Figures S2–S4). Their notebook additionally searches `random_state` 0–19 for the KMeans initialization that maximizes silhouette score, with an acceptance bar of silhouette > 0.25 in one code path.
- Paper's explicit framing (direct quote): *"In an effort to generate training and test sets that are distinct in chemical space, we used our pretrained model to conduct k-means clustering and used a leave-one-cluster-out approach for train-test splits with k-fold validation."* They also cite a specific motivating reason — a preprint showing scaffold-based splits overestimate generalization — as *why* they moved to ML-embedding clustering instead of scaffold splitting specifically.

### 1.3 What "leave-one-cluster-out" operationally means (this is the part worth getting exactly right)

Direct quote from the Methods section (paper text, not a paraphrase):

> *"We treated each cluster as an independent holdout set, iterating through all six clusters. For each test cluster, five models were trained on the remaining training clusters, using 5-fold cross-validation to predict permeability score... This training–prediction method was repeated six times, with each cluster used as the holdout test data one time... We predicted the permeability score on the holdout test cluster using the mean prediction from the k-fold models."*

So concretely: **it is a nested cross-validation scheme, not one fixed holdout and not a simple 6-fold CV.**
- **Outer loop (6 iterations):** each of the 6 clusters takes a turn as the fully-held-out test cluster.
- **Inner loop (5 models per outer iteration):** the other 5 clusters are used for 5-fold CV — cycling which cluster acts as the validation fold for early stopping (max 10,000 training steps, checkpoint selected by lowest validation MSE) while the remaining 4 clusters train.
- **Final test-cluster score** = mean of the predictions from those 5 inner models.
- Full reproduction is **up to 6 × 5 = 30 fine-tuning runs** per model architecture evaluated (they did this across 11 model variants in the comparison table — a real compute commitment, though each run is small: 6-layer, 768-hidden RoFormer, batch size 16, max 10,000 steps).
- I cross-checked this description against their own notebook code and it is consistent: the notebook constructs one outer iteration explicitly (one cluster fixed as the "validation" set, the other five rotating as "test" one at a time to produce `train_1..5`/`test_1..5` CSVs) — i.e., exactly the inner-5-fold-CV machinery described in the text, run once per choice of which cluster plays "test" vs "validation." Getting all 6 outer rotations requires re-running this construction 6 times with a different cluster fixed as validation each time.
- **Eval framing:** trained as continuous regression (MSE loss on Log P_exp), but *reported* both as regression (RMSE) and as binary classification (ROC-AUC, PR-AUC) after thresholding at Log P_exp = −5.5 (10⁻⁵·⁵ cm/s), the standard PAMPA permeability cutoff.
- Their own stated payoff: *"The better performance of PeptideCLM on the leave-one-cluster-out testing demonstrates the ability of the model to better generalize... more informative in laboratory scenarios where the available experimental data may not adequately cover the embedding space of all possible peptides."*

**One thing I could not verify:** the exact size (n) of the CycPeptMPDB PAMPA-subset used for fine-tuning. The paper states the pre-filter dataset (all of CycPeptMPDB) is ~7,500 cyclic peptides, and describes removing "undetectable" (−10) entries, but doesn't give the post-filter n in the portions of the main text I could access. This matters for calibrating k=6 apples-to-apples against this project's much smaller HC50/MIC sets (§3.4) — recommend pulling the actual "PAMPA cluster dataset" the authors link in their Data Availability statement (`github.com/AaronFeller/PeptideCLM`) to get the real number before committing to a k for this project.

## 2. QMAP's exact methodology (verified from primary sources)

**Sources used, fetched and read in full** (the first WebFetch attempts at the bioRxiv page and Nature mirror both 403'd on generic UA; a `curl` with a browser User-Agent succeeded and returned the full HTML, which was parsed to text and read in full):
- Preprint: Lavertu, A., Corbeil, J., Germain, P. *bioRxiv* 2026.02.03.703041 (posted 2026-02-04). `https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.02.03.703041v1.full`. Also published as Lavertu et al., *Scientific Reports* (2026), DOI `10.1038/s41598-026-56004-8` (paywalled mirror, not read directly — bioRxiv version used).
- Code: `github.com/anthol42/QMAP` — inspected file listing and read `QMAP/src/qmap/benchmark/benchmark.py` directly.
- Data: `huggingface.co/datasets/anthol42/qmap_benchmark_2025`.

### 2.1 Dataset scale (directly relevant to this project's small-n planning)

Built from **DBAASP v3** (Pirtskhalava et al. 2021, `10.1093/nar/gkaa991`), the same primary source `02_peptideclm_transfer_learning_plan.md` §5.3 recommends. After filtering to monomeric peptides, standard terminal modifications (N-acetyl, C-amide), canonical + a small allowlist of non-canonical residues (ornithine, DAB), and disulfide/amide-only intrachain bonds:
- **18,033 peptide sequences** total, spanning **492 bacterial species** for MIC.
- **~4,000 peptides** with MIC against *E. coli* specifically (the subset used in their model comparisons).
- **~800 peptides** with usable HC50 (human-erythrocyte-prioritized, IQR-outlier-filtered, mammalian-erythrocyte fallback).

**This is the single most load-bearing number for this project's planning:** even the best-resourced, most recent AMP-regression benchmark using the best available source (DBAASP) has an HC50 set of **only ~800 peptides**. This project's "low thousands at best" framing in the brief is optimistic for HC50 specifically unless supplemented (DRAMP/APD3/dbAMP/HemoPI, per §5.3) beyond what QMAP pulled — worth explicitly budgeting for n≈800–2,000 for HC50 splitting decisions, not n≈5,000.

### 2.2 Similarity metric — not CD-HIT, not MMseqs2

- **Global pairwise sequence identity** via **Needleman-Wunsch alignment**, parameterized with the **BLOSUM45** substitution matrix, gap-open penalty −5, gap-extend penalty −1.
- Metric ("maximum-identity"): for each test peptide, identity against *every* training peptide, keep the max. This yields one scalar per test peptide — the *max-identity distribution* — which is their leakage diagnostic (see §2.4, and reuse it in §5 below).
- They built a **custom Rust-accelerated pairwise-identity engine** ("pwiden," SIMD via the Parasail C library, multithreaded via Rust's `rayon`) rather than using CD-HIT or MMseqs2. The paper does not explicitly state *why* they avoided those tools (no direct quote saying "CD-HIT fails on short peptides") — **this causal claim is my inference, not a sourced fact, and should be flagged as such:** CD-HIT and MMseqs2 are both k-mer-seed-and-extend heuristics calibrated for full-length proteins (typical minimum word/k-mer sizes 2–7); at AMP lengths (many DBAASP entries are 5–25 residues), the k-mer becomes a large fraction of the whole sequence, which plausibly degrades their discriminative power relative to exact global alignment — but I did not find a source stating this directly for QMAP's specific choice. **Recommend empirically checking this on the project's own data** (§3.1) rather than assuming it.
  - Countervailing data point, found while checking the PeptideCLM-2 paper (§3.1 below): Feller's own group *does* use MMseqs2 successfully for peptide-length (≤100 aa) sequence deduplication in their pretraining-corpus construction (`--min-seq-id 0.3 -c 0.8 --cov-mode 1`), so MMseqs2 is not unusable at peptide length in general — it may specifically be less reliable at the very short end (5–20 aa) that dominates AMP libraries. Unresolved; test directly.
- **GraphPart** (Teufel et al., *NAR Genomics and Bioinformatics* 2023, DOI `10.1093/nargab/lqad088`, code `github.com/graph-part/graph-part`, `pip install graph-part`) is the tool QMAP explicitly built on top of / adapted — it is a ready-made, general-purpose implementation of exactly this "similarity graph → partition" idea, pluggable with different alignment backends. **This project doesn't need to reimplement QMAP's Rust engine — GraphPart is directly usable off the shelf**, or the released `qmap-benchmark` pip package can be used to enforce identity constraints against QMAP's own AMP-specific benchmark test sets if benchmarking against QMAP baselines is desired (per source doc §5.3's supplement note).

### 2.3 Partitioning algorithm (their adaptation, since vanilla GraphPart failed on DBAASP)

1. Build a similarity graph: node = peptide, edge if identity > threshold (60%, see §2.4).
2. **Applying vanilla GraphPart directly to DBAASP failed** — the dataset's similarity structure is dense enough that strict graph partitioning yielded an **empty test set**. This is a direct, concrete warning for this project: a "just run GraphPart" approach may not work out of the box on a DBAASP-derived AMP set either, and needs the same workaround.
3. Their fix: **community detection via the Leiden algorithm** (`leidenalg` package, modularity-based partition; Traag, Waltman & van Eck 2019, `10.1038/s41598-019-41695-z`) to group sequences into internally-connected communities, then **randomly assign whole communities** to the test set until the target test fraction is reached (GraphPart does this step deterministically; QMAP made it random specifically to allow generating multiple independent splits).
4. **Post-filter:** any training sequence whose identity to *any* test sequence exceeds the threshold is removed from training. (This is the step that trades away some training data for leakage safety — the actual mechanism, not just "cluster and split.")
5. **Test fraction: 20%.** **5 independent splits** generated via 5 different random seeds (1, 3, 7, 12, 404) — **not a rotating leave-one-out CV** the way Feller & Wilke's scheme is. Each of the 5 splits is used once, and performance variance *across* the 5 splits is reported (box plots, 5 measurements per model/metric), which is a materially cheaper compute pattern than Feller & Wilke's nested 30-run scheme — worth exploiting for this project (§3.5).
6. **HC50 uses "the same predefined test splits and independence constraints" as MIC** — confirmed both from the paper's prose and from the code: `QMAPBenchmark.__init__(split: int)` downloads one `benchmark_split_{split}.json` (0–4) and the same class (`DBAASPDataset` subclass) is filtered per task at evaluation time, i.e. **the underlying peptide-level graph partition is shared across MIC and HC50, not independently re-derived per task.** This is direct evidence for the joint-split recommendation in §3.3.
7. Important asymmetry in QMAP's design: **only the test sets are fixed/released**; users bring their *own* training data and use `get_train_mask()` to filter out anything too similar (>60% identity) to the chosen test split. This is a reasonable pattern for a public benchmark (lets new training data get incorporated without invalidating comparisons) but is not quite what this project needs — this project needs a full train/val/test partition of its *own* dataset, so treat QMAP's masking utility as a candidate implementation, not literally reuse their released test sets, unless choosing to additionally benchmark against QMAP (worth doing per source doc §5.3, but as a distinct exercise from this project's own primary split).

### 2.4 Threshold: 60% identity — and exactly how they derived it

- **Empirical reference distribution:** max-identity between **PeptideAtlas** (Desiere et al. 2006, `10.1093/nar/gkj040` — the largest natural-peptide sequence collection, used as a proxy for "peptides a deployed model might see") and DBAASP. **99th percentile ≈ 61.5%.**
- They chose a threshold of **60%** — "slightly more conservative" than that 99th percentile — reasoning that deployment-time sequences will typically be *at least* this dissimilar from the curated training set, so evaluating at this threshold approximates real deployment-time generalization difficulty.
- **Direct evidence the threshold matters (their Fig. 3A):** a linear probe on **ESM2-650M** embeddings, trained/evaluated at varying identity thresholds, shows Pearson correlation coefficient **increasing monotonically with threshold** — i.e., as you allow more train/test homology, apparent performance goes up, which is leakage made visible as a curve, not just an assertion.
- **Direct evidence of the magnitude of random-split leakage (their Fig. 1), the single most quotable number for this project:**
  - **Homology-aware split:** median max-identity(test→train) = **50.0%**, 90th percentile = **57.1%**.
  - **Naive random split:** median = **90.0%**, 90th percentile = **100.0%** (i.e., under random splitting, the *median* test peptide has a near-duplicate in training).
  - This is on the *same* underlying DBAASP-derived dataset — a clean, direct A/B, not a comparison across different datasets.

## 3. Concrete splitting pipeline for this project

### 3.1 Embedding choice: PeptideCLM-2 mean-pooled final layer (not v1's CLS-token convention)

**Important correction/clarification to the source doc's §5.4,** which just says "cluster with the PeptideCLM-2 embeddings themselves" without specifying pooling — and to §5.6's illustrative code, which already guesses mean pooling (`last_hidden_state.mean(dim=1)`).

I checked this directly against the PeptideCLM-2 paper itself (bioRxiv `10.64898/2026.01.06.697994`; note — **the preprint's title has drifted across versions**: v1 is titled *"PeptideMTR: Scaling SMILES-Based Language Models for Therapeutic Peptide Engineering,"* v2/v3 are titled *"Scaling SMILES-Based Chemical Language Models for Therapeutic Peptide Engineering"* — same DOI prefix, same authors (Feller et al.), same HF org `aaronfeller`; flag this naming drift so it doesn't cause confusion when citing it later. Free full text: PMC12803269.)

Confirmed from that paper's own Methods, multiple places: *"the multi-task regression (MTR) objective explicitly grounded the embeddings with regression to 99 RDKit-derived physicochemical descriptors from a **mean-pooled embedding**"*; downstream probing used *"**final-layer mean-pooled embeddings**"*; layer-wise probing used embeddings *"mean-pooled across the sequence dimension."* **PeptideCLM-2's own convention is mean pooling of the final hidden layer — the opposite of v1's CLS-token convention used for the original leave-one-cluster-out clustering.** Since §5.2 of the source doc already selects PeptideCLM-2 as the primary model, **use mean pooling** (matches both PeptideCLM-2's own convention and the source doc's own §5.6 code sketch — no change needed there, just now confirmed correct rather than assumed).

**Concrete recipe (reusing Feller & Wilke's dimensionality-reduction choice, which is model-agnostic and reasonable to reuse even though the pooling differs):**
1. Encode every unique peptide (dedupe on canonical SMILES first) through the **base pretrained** PeptideCLM-2 encoder (hybrid-small variant per §5.2) — not a fine-tuned checkpoint, to avoid biasing clusters toward any one downstream task.
2. Extract mean-pooled final-hidden-layer embeddings.
3. PCA to 99% variance retained (Feller & Wilke's exact threshold; re-derive the component count for this dataset — likely far fewer than their 153 given a smaller, less chemically diverse dataset).
4. K-means, sweep k (see §3.4 for why k should be smaller here than their k=6), select via silhouette + Davies-Bouldin + Calinski-Harabasz jointly, exactly as they did — this is a well-motivated, reproducible selection rule worth carrying over verbatim.

**Open question to verify before implementing:** check the actual HF model card(s) under `huggingface.co/collections/aaronfeller/peptideclm-2` for any explicitly recommended embedding-extraction snippet — I did not fetch the HF pages directly, only the paper text, so it's possible the released inference code defaults differently than what the paper's own probing experiments used.

### 3.2 Homology-based clustering as an independent cross-check

Run a **second, independent** split using sequence-identity graph partitioning, not as a replacement but as the "consistency check" flagged in the task brief:

- **Tool:** `graph-part` (pip-installable, `github.com/graph-part/graph-part`) as the primary choice — it's the same lineage QMAP built on, actively maintained, and avoids reimplementing Leiden-community-detection-plus-post-filter logic from scratch. Fall back to reimplementing QMAP's Leiden-plus-random-community-assignment-plus-post-filter approach (as GraphPart itself failed on raw DBAASP, per §2.3) if GraphPart's own community-merging heuristics still yield a degenerate (near-empty) test set on this project's smaller data — a real risk given QMAP hit exactly this failure mode on the *larger* DBAASP set.
- **Alignment method:** global Needleman-Wunsch, not CD-HIT/MMseqs2 k-mer seeding, given the short-peptide concern in §2.2 — but **empirically compare** CD-HIT-2D and/or MMseqs2 clustering against the NW-based one on this project's actual peptide length distribution before ruling them out; they're much faster if the dataset grows, and the "unreliable at short length" concern is inference, not a verified fact.
- **Threshold:** don't blindly reuse QMAP's 60% — that number was derived from *their* PeptideAtlas-vs-DBAASP reference distribution, which is dataset-specific by construction (§2.4). Re-derive the equivalent 99th-percentile reference number for this project's own dataset (PeptideAtlas or a similar broad peptide collection vs. this project's assembled HC50/MIC set) before picking a threshold. If time-constrained, 60% is a defensible starting point given the substantial dataset overlap (DBAASP as primary source in both cases, per source doc §5.3).
- **Use as a diagnostic, not a tie-breaker:** compute the agreement (e.g., adjusted Rand index) between the embedding-based cluster labels and the homology-graph community labels. High agreement is reassuring (both methods are finding the same underlying chemical-family structure). Low agreement is not necessarily a bug — embedding clusters can legitimately separate on axes (net charge, amphipathicity, length) that pure sequence identity doesn't capture and that plausibly matter more for a permeabilization/lysis task specifically — but it should be *understood and reported*, not silently ignored. Recommend the **final split** be the sequence-identity-graph partition (since it has the more rigorous, directly-measurable leakage guarantee — max-identity is bounded by construction, whereas embedding-cluster separation is only as good as the encoder's geometry), with the embedding-cluster split reported as a secondary/reported check, unless the agreement analysis finds strong reason to prefer the embedding clusters (e.g., much better silhouette separation with comparably low max-identity leakage in the §5 validation step).

### 3.3 Joint vs. independent splitting for HC50 and MIC — split jointly, at the peptide level

This is the one place the source doc's §5.4 doesn't give an explicit answer, and where getting it wrong would quietly reintroduce the exact leakage problem §5.4 exists to prevent — because of the **shared-encoder multitask architecture from §5.1.**

**Concrete rule: build one master peptide list (the union of every peptide with an HC50 label, an MIC label, or both), run clustering/partitioning once on that master list, and derive per-task train/val/test sets by filtering that single partition down to whichever peptides have that task's label.** Do not cluster/partition HC50 and MIC separately and then combine.

**Why this is required, not just tidy:** QMAP's own precedent (§2.3, point 6) already does exactly this — one shared partition, filtered per task at eval time — which is corroborating evidence this is the right pattern for a joint MIC/HC50 benchmark specifically. But there's a *stronger* reason specific to this project's architecture: §5.1 proposes a **shared encoder** across HC50 and MIC heads, trained with a masked multitask loss so "peptides with only one label still train the shared trunk." If peptide X is in the HC50 test set but also happens to be in the MIC training set, the shared encoder still sees X's chemistry during MIC training and updates its representation of X's neighborhood in embedding space — which can leak into HC50 test performance even though X's *HC50 label* was never used in training. Splitting only "per-task" (i.e., independently clustering/partitioning the HC50-labeled peptides and the MIC-labeled peptides) does not prevent this, because it doesn't prevent a peptide from landing in opposite roles across the two tasks. A joint, peptide-level split is the only way to guarantee a peptide's cluster membership — and therefore its train/val/test role — is identical no matter which head(s) use it.

Practical note: DBAASP peptides with *both* HC50 and MIC annotations are exactly the peptides the therapeutic-index (TI) framing in §5.1 cares about most, so this joint split also naturally keeps TI-relevant dual-labeled peptides consistently in-fold.

### 3.4 Train/val/test proportions and cluster count given small n

Reference numbers to plan against (from §2.1, and worth re-measuring on this project's actual pull at P0 rather than assumed): **HC50 ≈ 800–2,000 peptides** (QMAP's DBAASP-only pull got 800; supplementing with DRAMP/APD3/dbAMP/HemoPI per source doc §5.3 should raise this, but by an unknown amount — flag as an open question resolved only once the P0 data build finishes), **MIC ≈ several thousand** peptides (QMAP's E. coli-only cut was ~4,000; a broader bacterial panel per source doc §6.4 Q1 could be larger but reintroduces species heterogeneity).

Given this:
- **k should be smaller than Feller & Wilke's k=6**, especially for HC50. Their k=6 was tuned on their own PAMPA-finetuning dataset (size unverified, §1.3, but CycPeptMPDB-scale, plausibly several thousand peptides) — spreading ~800 HC50 peptides over 6 clusters risks single-digit-to-low-double-digit test-cluster sizes for the smallest cluster, which makes per-fold Spearman ρ / MAE extremely noisy (a handful of points can swing rank correlation wildly). **Recommend k = 4–5 for HC50, k = 5–6 for MIC**, chosen via the same silhouette/DB/CH sweep, but explicitly rejecting any k whose smallest resulting cluster falls below a floor (suggest ≥ 40–60 peptides, enough for a minimally stable Spearman ρ estimate) rather than picking k purely by the unsupervised cluster-quality metrics.
- **Proportions:** LOCO-style splitting doesn't let you dial in an exact train/val/test percentage the way a stratified random split would — cluster sizes are whatever k-means produces. As a planning approximation, k=5 LOCO gives ~20% test per fold (matching QMAP's chosen 20% test fraction, reassuringly), k=4 gives ~25%. Reserve one additional cluster (or the smallest cluster within the non-test set, following Feller & Wilke's notebook pattern of fixing one cluster as validation) for early-stopping validation, leaving the rest for training. Net effective split is roughly **train ≈ 55–70% / val ≈ 15–20% / test ≈ 20–25%** per fold, varying by which cluster is held out — report the actual per-fold sizes rather than assuming they're balanced, and treat large fold-to-fold size imbalance itself as a data point (§5).
- **For the HC50 head specifically**, given n≈800–2,000 is genuinely small, also budget for **k-fold-only evaluation without a fully separate validation cluster** (i.e., use the remaining 4 clusters directly for training with an internal random validation carve-out, rather than sacrificing a whole cluster to validation) as a fallback if the validation-cluster approach leaves too little data to train a stable head. This is a real trade-off to make explicitly, not silently default on.

### 3.5 CV scheme and compute cost — recommend a cheaper hybrid, not Feller & Wilke's exact 30-run nested scheme

Source doc §6.1 already notes fine-tuning at this scale is cheap ("comfortably on a single Colab/consumer GPU"). Given that, running the *full* Feller & Wilke nested scheme (k outer × 5 inner) is affordable in wall-clock terms — but it's not obviously the best use of that compute budget at this n, because:

- With n≈800–2,000 and k=4–5, each "remaining 4 clusters" training set is already ≥75–80% of the full data — the marginal difference between the 5 inner-CV folds (each excluding a further ~20% of the *already-small* training pool for validation) is large relative to n, meaning the inner models are trained on meaningfully less data than a single well-chosen validation split would need to sacrifice.
- QMAP's alternative (5 independent seeded test-holdout splits, no nested inner CV, report variance across the 5) is a materially cheaper pattern that still gets you a variance estimate across splits — just not a variance estimate *per split* the way nested CV does.

**Recommendation: a hybrid.** Run the **outer LOCO rotation** (k = 4–5 clusters, each rotating through as the fully-held-out test set — this is the leakage-critical part, keep it) but replace the **inner 5-fold CV with a single fixed validation cluster** for early stopping/checkpoint selection (exactly the pattern already visible in Feller & Wilke's own notebook code, which fixes one cluster as "validation" while rotating the rest as "test" — i.e., use their own simplification rather than the full nested description in the paper prose). This needs **k fine-tuning runs total** (one per outer test-cluster rotation, e.g. 4–5 runs) instead of up to 30, at the cost of a somewhat noisier per-fold validation signal — an acceptable trade at this n, and revisit (add back inner-fold ensembling) only if per-fold variance in the reported metrics turns out to be uncomfortably high once measured.

Compute estimate: PeptideCLM-2 hybrid-small (tens of millions of params) fine-tuning a regression head on ≤2,000 SMILES sequences, frozen-encoder warm-up + light unfreeze (§5.5 P2 plan) — each run should be on the order of minutes to low tens of minutes on a single consumer/Colab-class GPU. 4–6 outer folds × 2 tasks (HC50, MIC) × the P3 multitask variant ≈ well under an hour of total GPU time for the core LOCO evaluation; the 5-independent-QMAP-style-splits alternative is comparable or cheaper. Neither option is a real compute constraint at this project's scale — the choice above is about statistical efficiency given small n, not affordability.

## 4. Cyclic-holdout ablation (source doc §6.1)

### 4.1 Identifying "a cyclic cluster" concretely

Two complementary approaches — do both, and check whether they agree (itself informative, see §4.3):

1. **Metadata-defined (primary, preferred):** use the modification metadata built in §5.3's data build. DBAASP (and QMAP's DBAASP-derived formatting, confirmed from their Methods text) encodes this via an **intrachain-bond field** — a peptide is backbone- or side-chain-cyclized if it has a bond connecting its N-/C-termini or a side chain to a terminus (the "amide bond" intrachain-bond category QMAP explicitly names, as distinct from disulfide bonds, which link two residues without necessarily closing the whole backbone into a ring). Concretely: flag `is_cyclic = True` for any peptide with a head-to-tail or side-chain-to-tail intrachain amide bond in the source structural annotation; treat disulfide-only peptides as a separate, intermediate category (constrained but not backbone-cyclized) rather than lumping them in with either linear or cyclic, since the mechanism (and PeptideCLM's SMILES-visible chemistry) differs.
2. **Embedding-cluster-derived (secondary, exploratory):** since ring closure changes SMILES topology substantially, it's plausible that PeptideCLM-2's embedding space naturally separates cyclic from linear peptides into distinct k-means clusters without being told to — this is literally the closest concrete reading of source doc §6.1's "hold out a cyclic cluster" phrasing (i.e., check whether *one of the unsupervised clusters from §3.1 already is, predominantly, the cyclic peptides* — rather than constructing an artificial cyclic/linear boundary). Cross-tabulate the metadata `is_cyclic` flag against the k-means cluster assignments once both exist; if one cluster is strongly cyclic-enriched, that's the natural candidate to additionally single out as the designated cyclic-holdout fold within the LOCO scheme, rather than building a separate ablation split.

### 4.2 Fraction of data this removes

**Could not verify a concrete number from any source read for this task.** Neither the QMAP paper nor the Feller & Wilke paper reports what fraction of a DBAASP-derived AMP set is cyclic (QMAP's filtering criteria — "no intrachain bonds... or commonly occurring intrachain bonds, such as disulfide and amidation bonds" — describes *which* cyclic peptides they kept, not what fraction of the total that is). **This is a number that can only be answered by measuring it directly on this project's own P0 data pull**, and should be reported as part of the P0 datasheet deliverable the source doc already calls for. Budget for the possibility that it's a small minority (cyclic AMP *lysis* data being described as "scarce" is exactly source doc §6.2's own framing) — if the cyclic-flagged subset is too small (say, under ~30–50 peptides) to be a statistically meaningful held-out test set on its own, fall back to reporting it as a *qualitative* case-study / smallest-cluster comparison rather than a formal CV fold, and say so explicitly rather than presenting underpowered numbers as if they were.

### 4.3 What comparison constitutes evidence of the domain-shift risk

1. Train on **all clusters except the cyclic-flagged one(s)** — i.e., add the cyclic-holdout as one more rotation of the LOCO scheme from §3.5, rather than a bespoke one-off split, so it's directly comparable to the "ordinary" (majority-linear) folds using the same training procedure.
2. Compute the same metrics (Spearman ρ, Pearson r, MAE/RMSE, per source doc §5.5 P4) on:
   - (a) each of the "ordinary" LOCO test folds (mostly linear, in-distribution by construction of the training-data skew), and
   - (b) the cyclic-holdout fold.
3. **Evidence of domain shift = the cyclic-fold metric falling outside the range (or below mean − ~2 SD) of the ordinary-fold metrics.** Concretely: take the distribution of per-fold Spearman ρ across the (k−1) ordinary LOCO folds as the "normal generalization-noise" reference range; if the cyclic fold's ρ is below the minimum of that range (or more formally, below mean − 2×SD, acknowledging k=4–5 gives a very rough SD estimate), that's evidence the cyclic/linear domain shift is a *distinct, additional* generalization penalty beyond ordinary cluster-to-cluster variability — which is the specific claim source doc §6.1 flags as a risk worth quantifying, not just asserting. If cyclic-fold performance falls within the ordinary range, the honest conclusion is that the encoder's cyclic-pretraining exposure (per source doc §6.2, PeptideCLM-2 was pretrained on a mix including cyclic peptides) is generalizing fine for lysis specifically, and the concern doesn't need to gate anything downstream.
4. Report this **per task** (HC50 and MIC separately) — there's no reason to assume the domain-shift magnitude is the same for both, and a difference between them would itself be an interesting finding (e.g., if MIC's larger n makes it more robust to the shift than HC50's smaller n).

## 5. Post-split leakage validation — measure, don't assume

The task brief specifically asks for this to be a measured check, not an assumed consequence of "we used clustering." Concretely:

1. **Reproduce QMAP's max-identity(test → train) diagnostic directly on this project's final split(s).** For every test peptide, global-alignment identity (same BLOSUM45/−5/−1 NW parameters QMAP used, for direct comparability) against every training peptide, keep the max, plot the distribution, report median and 90th percentile. **Benchmark against QMAP's own published reference numbers** as a sanity check: this project's split should land near their homology-split numbers (median ≈ 50%, 90th pctile ≈ 57%), not their random-split numbers (median ≈ 90%, 90th pctile ≈ 100%) — even though this project's primary split is embedding-cluster-based rather than sequence-identity-based, which is exactly why this check matters: embedding clusters are not *guaranteed* to be homology-safe just because they're not random, and this is the direct test of whether they actually are.
2. **Embedding-space nearest-neighbor check**, as a complement to the sequence-identity one (and cheap to compute, since the embeddings already exist from §3.1): for each test peptide, cosine or Euclidean distance to its nearest training-set neighbor in embedding space; compare the within-cluster vs. cross-cluster nearest-neighbor distance distributions. This diagnoses whether the k-means clusters are actually well-separated in the sense that matters (test points aren't near-neighbors of train points), which silhouette score summarizes but a full distance histogram makes visible and auditable.
3. **Reproduce QMAP's threshold-sensitivity experiment (their Fig. 3A) on this project's own data**, not just cite theirs: train a cheap baseline (linear probe or ElasticNet on frozen PeptideCLM-2 embeddings, matching QMAP's own baseline choice of a linear model on frozen embeddings for exactly this kind of diagnostic) across a range of identity thresholds (e.g., 100% [~random], 80%, 60%, 40%) and confirm apparent PCC/Spearman ρ **rises as threshold rises** — and report the actual magnitude of the effect *on this dataset*, since QMAP's own number (their Fig. 1 median-90-vs-50 gap) is dataset- and task-specific and shouldn't be assumed to transfer directly to this project's HC50/MIC data.
4. **Label-distribution balance check across folds:** for both HC50 and MIC, confirm the label distribution (mean, variance, min/max of log-transformed values) is comparable across train/val/test for every LOCO fold. Because k-means clusters on chemistry, not on the label, it's entirely possible for a cluster to accidentally concentrate unusually potent or unusually toxic peptides (e.g., a cluster of highly cationic short peptides could skew toward extreme MIC/HC50 values) — this would make that fold's held-out metrics uninterpretable (too easy or too hard) independent of any leakage question. If found, treat as a legitimate reason to re-roll the clustering random seed (already part of the k-means selection procedure in §3.1/§3.4) with label-balance added as a secondary selection criterion, not just cluster-quality metrics.
5. **Cyclic/linear and dual-labeled (HC50+MIC) composition per fold**, for the same reason as point 4 — report what fraction of each fold is cyclic and what fraction is dual-labeled, so downstream metric interpretation (§4.3, and the TI-ranking metric in source doc §5.5 P4) isn't confounded by an accidentally lopsided fold.
